# Competition changes things

The over-saturation of content means that creators have to try really hard to (a) attain and (b) maintain visibility. This, I would argue, has led to a standardisation of content. Suddenly, every video you see has an algorithm hook or attention-grabbing editing style to boost engagement. There are outliers, but I can often tell when the creator is more focused on the numbers rather than their subject. There’s nothing inherently wrong with that, but it changes things (the tone, pacing, etc.).

That is why I’m not convinced that human-made content will become more valuable in the age of AI. In theory, it does become more intrinsically valuable. It should matter that someone has put in the effort to create something. However, if they are competing with AI and humans, can they make something authentic if their platform of choice demands conformity to these standards to stay relevant?

Let’s face it, creators will be leveraging AI to raise their profile, even if they aren’t using generative AI to produce content. This will put further pressure on creators to assimilate. I believe that opting out of these practices is a noble choice, but if it reaches a point where being real and personable loses you access to your prospective audience, it might be time to consider an alternative route.

# AI Training, YouTube’s Terms of Service

There’s also the principled concern of your content being used to train AI. When you upload a video to YouTube, for example, you grant Google [a broad license to use your content to improve their services](https://www.billboard.com/pro/google-youtube-terms-of-service-ai-music-training-lawsuit/). It’s open to interpretation, but making your content available to the parent company seems to be part of the deal, even though you retain “ownership rights”. So, not only are you being asked to participate in a competitive attention economy, you are also implicated in practices you might not necessarily agree with. That is the price of entry to YouTube’s unrivalled ecosystem.

# Dead internet theory

Even if you accept these conditions and become successful, do you ever really know who your audience is? Dead internet theory broadly discusses the idea that the internet [consists primarily of bot activity and automated content](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dead_Internet_theory). With the onboarding of [agentic AI](https://about.fb.com/news/2026/09/introducing-muse-personal-ai-agent/), or AI models that act on your behalf, this is going to become an even bigger problem. It is already difficult to discern actual human engagement from bot activity. Eventually, we now have to worry about AI agents [arguing with each other in the comment section](https://moltsbooks.com/), at which point the internet might well be dead. It shouldn’t be about viewership or subscriber count, but if nobody sees your content, why tolerate all of the downsides of signing up?

# What is the alternative?

The internet is changing. As I mentioned, the algorithm-isation of content makes it difficult to adopt or maintain an old school approach to content creation. There's no doubt that longform, casual videos will continue to appeal to those who are looking for relatable content. However, the question is: are big tech platforms becoming increasingly hostile towards these creators via algorithmic search functions and the rapid implementation of AI? I suspect the answer is yes, particularly if you don't already have an established fanbase.

For a smaller creator, like me, I'm now looking elsewhere. I have had a good experience with Bluesky, the decentralised Twitter/X alternative. It gives you more agency over what you see. I haven't exclusively posted content there, but I have connected with like-minded people who also have niche interests. I have also looked into self-hosting content on my own website. These are just some of the alternatives I'm considering in response to these changes.
